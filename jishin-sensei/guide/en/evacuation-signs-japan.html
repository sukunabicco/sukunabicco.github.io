How to read Japan's evacuation signs: the ○/× marks and the green pictograms

The signs are everywhere; most visitors walk past them. Four questions from the deck, with the official sources.

🪧 After the quake you reach the park an evacuation sign pointed to. On the sign, a row of small square symbols — and under the one with the wave there is an ×.

What is that × under the wave telling you?

✅ The right move: This site is NOT for tsunami — if a tsunami threatens, keep going to higher ground

Why: An evacuation site is designated per disaster, and the row of symbols is the sign saying so: flood, debris flow, tsunami and storm surge, landslide, large fire. A ○ under a symbol means the site handles that disaster; an × means it does not — a riverside park can be the right place in a big fire and the wrong place in a flood or tsunami. The symbols were standardized nationwide in 2016, so they read the same in every town. Glance at them as you arrive: in a tsunami what counts is height, and the place to be is one marked for the wave — or simply higher ground.

Source: Cabinet Office — standardized symbols for evacuation sites (Japanese page)

🟩 Fires have broken out after the quake and an officer waves you off the block. Two green signs point different ways: a person running toward a circle, and a person inside a building.

Which sign do you follow right now?

✅ The right move: The running figure — an emergency evacuation area, open ground where you go while the danger is live

Why: Two different signs with two different jobs. The running figure marks an emergency evacuation area: open ground where you go to survive the danger of the moment — a spreading fire, a collapsing block. The building figure marks an evacuation shelter: a school or community center where people live for a while after their homes are lost. The shelter is not necessarily open or away from the hazard, so while the danger is live you follow the running figure; the building sign matters later, if you cannot go back to where you were staying. Both welcome travelers.

Source: Disaster Preparedness Tokyo (Tokyo Metropolitan Government)

🏫 After a major earthquake, signs point to a 'Designated Evacuation Shelter' at a nearby school.

What is a Designated Evacuation Shelter?

✅ The right move: A facility like a school or community center, run by the local government, where people stay until the danger passes

Why: Designated Evacuation Shelters are public facilities — often schools and community centers — operated by local governments for people whose safety is threatened by a disaster. Visitors can seek help there too; follow the staff's guidance.

Source: Safety tips for travelers (Japan Tourism Agency / JNTO)

🌅 You're strolling along a seaside promenade. The ground begins to sway — gently, but it goes on for a strangely long time.

The shaking was weak. What should you do?

✅ The right move: Start evacuating to higher ground immediately — long shaking near the coast is a sign of a tsunami

Why: Official guidance is explicit: when there is a strong — or weak but long-lasting — earthquake near the coast, start evacuating. A slow, long swaying can mean a large, distant earthquake capable of generating a tsunami. Don't wait for the official warning to begin moving.

Source: Safety tips for travelers (Japan Tourism Agency / JNTO)
